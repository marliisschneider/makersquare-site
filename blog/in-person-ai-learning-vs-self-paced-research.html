Why in-person AI learning produces better outcomes than self-paced: what the research shows.

Marliis Schneider July 8, 2026 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • Self-paced online courses average 5–15% completion rates — not because of motivation, but because the format is structurally mismatched with complex skill development.
  • Three mechanisms explain why in-person learning produces better outcomes: spaced repetition with immediate feedback, social accountability, and project-based application.
  • AI is specifically hard to learn self-paced because the feedback loop is weak — you don't know if your prompts are good until you've built something real and compared outputs with people who know the difference.
  • Self-paced is the right format for building conceptual foundation; in-person intensive is the right format for building applied skill quickly.

Most people who decide to "learn AI" start with a YouTube video or a free course. A meaningful percentage stick with it long enough to finish one module. A smaller percentage finish the course. A smaller percentage still apply what they learned to their actual work.

This is not a motivation problem. It is a format problem. And the research on how people learn complex skills is clear about why self-paced formats underperform — and what works better.

The completion rate problem is structural

HarvardX and MITx research on massive open online courses found that self-paced online courses average 5–15% completion rates. Jordan's IRRODL study on self-paced online courses found similar numbers — with the drop-off concentrated in the first two weeks. These numbers have remained roughly stable since MOOCs launched over a decade ago, despite improvements in content quality, platform design, and learner support.

The pattern holds because the structural problem is the same: self-paced learning competes with everything else in a person's day, and the immediate cost of skipping a module is zero. The commitment device that would make someone complete a self-paced course — the thing that makes them show up when the friction is high — doesn't exist in the format. There is no cohort waiting. No instructor who will notice the absence. No project due date. No social consequence for stopping.

The people who do complete self-paced courses and transfer the skills to real work tend to have unusual self-discipline, an existing project that creates immediate application, or a peer group that provides external accountability. These are the exceptions, not the format working as designed.

Three learning mechanisms that in-person delivers

Spaced repetition with immediate feedback

Cepeda et al.'s research on spaced repetition found that distributing practice across time significantly outperforms massed practice for long-term retention. A well-structured intensive program — where you learn something, apply it in a project the same day, revisit it from a different angle the next morning, and encounter it again in a capstone project in week two — is applying spaced repetition across a compressed timeline. Self-paced learning often produces massed practice around exam windows, which produces lower retention.

The feedback loop matters even more than the spacing. Immediate, specific feedback on your work is how you know whether you understood something correctly or have a misunderstanding that will compound. In self-paced formats, the feedback is a grade on a quiz. In in-person settings, it's an instructor watching you build and pointing out in real time why the prompt you just wrote produced the wrong output.

Social accountability

BCG's research on AI skill development found that professionals in cohort-based learning environments apply what they learn at higher rates than self-directed learners. Part of this is social accountability — the cohort creates a peer group that notices when you're stuck, provides context from their own experience, and creates mild social pressure to complete the work. Part of it is the network itself: the relationships formed in an intensive program tend to persist, which means you have people to compare notes with after the program ends.

This is not a soft benefit. The peer cohort is one of the highest-rated outcomes by MakerSquare alumni — specifically because working professionals don't have many other ways to find a group of people building similar things with similar constraints.

Project-based application

The most important predictor of skill transfer is whether you build something real. Not an exercise with a predetermined correct answer — a project you designed, that has to work on real data, that has to hold up to your own standards. In self-paced formats, this is possible but rare. In in-person intensive programs, it's the entire structure of the second week.

Pearson's workforce research found that professionals who build AI-powered tools during their training apply AI skills 3x more frequently in the months after training than those who completed conceptual-only programs. The project is what makes the skill portable.

Why AI is specifically harder to learn self-paced

AI tools change faster than courses update. A self-paced course on Claude that was recorded six months ago is already partially outdated. Learners have no mechanism to know what's current and what isn't — they're relying on the course to be accurate, which is a problem when the tools themselves are updated monthly.

The skill of directing AI well is also tacit in a way that makes it hard to describe in a video. You can watch someone write a good prompt and understand it. But knowing whether your own prompts are good requires seeing the difference between what a good prompt produces and what a bad one produces — over and over, with someone who can explain the gap. That feedback loop doesn't exist in self-paced formats.

When self-paced makes sense

Self-paced is the right starting point for building conceptual foundation — understanding how large language models work, what the key vocabulary means, and whether AI is relevant to your work before committing to a more intensive format. It is also the right format for professionals with genuine self-discipline, an existing project to apply learning to immediately, and a peer group that can provide external accountability.

For professionals who need to build real skills quickly and apply them to actual work — the typical MakerSquare student — the evidence points clearly toward structured in-person learning. The full curriculum shows what that looks like day by day.

Frequently asked questions
Why do most people fail to learn AI on their own?
Self-paced online courses average 5–15% completion rates. The problem isn't motivation — it's structure. Without a fixed schedule, peer accountability, or immediate feedback on applied work, most people deprioritize learning under the pressure of daily work. It's not a personal failure; it's a format mismatch for complex skill development.
Is in-person AI training worth it compared to online courses?
For professionals who need to apply skills quickly and build real projects, in-person training consistently outperforms self-paced online courses on completion rates, skill transfer, and time-to-application. The higher cost reflects the higher completion rate and the project you leave with — not just the information itself.
What makes AI specifically hard to learn self-paced?
AI tools change fast — courses go stale quickly and self-directed learners have no mechanism to know what's current. The feedback loop in self-paced learning is weak: you don't know if your prompts are good until you've built something real and compared outputs with people who know the difference. And the skill of directing AI well is tacit — it's absorbed through practice with feedback, not from reading about it.
When does self-paced AI learning make sense?
Self-paced works well for building conceptual foundation before a more intensive program, for exploring whether AI is relevant to your work before investing further, and for professionals with strong self-discipline and an existing project to apply learning to immediately. It's not the right format for building deep applied skill quickly — but it's a reasonable starting point.
