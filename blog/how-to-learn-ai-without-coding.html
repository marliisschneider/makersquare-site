How to learn AI without being a coder

Marliis Schneider Updated November 21, 2026 6 min read
Key takeaways
  • You don’t need to learn to code first. Start by using ChatGPT or Claude on tasks from your own week.
  • Once you’re comfortable, you can build tools without writing code. You describe what you want in plain English, and Claude Code writes the code.
  • Learn on real work, not videos. Pick one task you do every week and get better at it with AI.

People ask us some version of this all the time: “Can I learn AI without being a coder?” Yes.

In 2015, Shaan, one of our co-founders, spent three months in a full-time coding program so he could build software himself. Back then, that’s what it took. Today our students build working tools for their own jobs in two weeks, some in a single weekend, and most of them have never written code.

You can learn AI in two steps. Step 1 is using it: you ask ChatGPT or Claude to help with work you already do, like writing a client email or summarizing a meeting. Step 2 is building with it: you make a small tool that does one of those tasks for you every time, without you asking. You don’t write code for either one.

Step 1: Get good at using AI on your real work

Open ChatGPT or Claude and use it on something you already do every week: a client email, a meeting summary, a report, research before a call.

Most people type “write me a follow-up email” and get something generic back. The people who get good at it give AI what they’d give a new hire:

  • Context. Who it’s for, what happened before, and what you want the person to do next.
  • An example. Paste in an email or report you liked and say “write it like this.”
  • Your rules. How long, what tone, and what to never say.

Then read what comes back the way a manager would. You know your field, so you’ll catch what it gets wrong (a wrong date, a promise you can’t make).

Maybe you’re already further along. You use Projects in Claude, or Projects and custom GPTs in ChatGPT, so your instructions and examples are saved and you don’t paste them in every time. That’s using AI well, and it’s the base for step 2.

Step 2: Build tools without writing code

Even with a good Project, you still open the chat, ask, and copy the answer to wherever it goes, one task at a time. A tool you build does those steps for you. It starts on its own (a new email arrives, or it’s Monday at 8 AM). It works inside your real tools like Gmail, Outlook or your CRM, and puts the result where it belongs.

You don’t write the code. With Claude Code, you describe what you want in plain English, and it writes and runs the code. Your job is to explain the task clearly, test what it built, and tell it what to fix. (We go deeper on the difference in Building With AI vs. Using AI.)

Here’s what people with no coding background have built:

  • Jennifer Francis (construction project manager): every RFI and submittal email meant four manual steps. Her Outlook agent now downloads the file, renames it to her naming convention, saves it, and logs it in the right project hub for her to confirm.
  • Alex Altman (runs finance, had never written code): monthly revenue reporting took four hours, so it kept getting put off. Now it takes one click and comes with a report ready for auditors. Along the way, the tool caught two errors in the company’s books.
  • Wesley Bedwell (6th-grade teacher): teachers scan a stack of handwritten work. The tool grades each page against the teacher’s own rubric and drafts feedback for every student, saving an estimated 4–6 hours of grading a week.
  • A sales rep on Anthropic’s Japan team: he spent a couple of hours each morning sorting leads and writing emails. He assumed agents were for developers, then described each one’s job in plain language. Now his agents sort leads, draft replies, update Salesforce and write meeting follow-ups before his day starts. He reviews everything.
  • Dr. Yoojin Nam (radiology resident, no coding background): about 80% of research time went to repetitive steps, like downloading the PDFs for 76 studies one by one. That first tool grew into 22, one for each step repeated on every project.
  • Chris Cox (Hesed House, a homeless shelter): his agent reads emails about shelter guests and pulls out a record. Then it matches the record to the right guest on a dashboard for case managers. When it isn’t sure, it flags the record for a person to check.
  • A legal professional in Japan (judicial scrivener, no programming background): the case software cost about ¥12,000 a month and didn’t fit the work. In about 25 hours, they built their own case tracker with deadline alerts and a document checklist for each type of case.

Each one started as a task the person already did by hand. There are more on our student projects page.

The 4 skills that matter (none of them are coding)

  • Explaining a task clearly. If you can write the steps down for a new hire, you can describe them to AI.
  • Breaking work into steps. “Read the email, grab the file, rename it, save it” is easier to build (and fix) than “handle my inbox.”
  • Checking the output. You know your field better than any AI does. That’s what catches the mistakes.
  • Picking the right task. Start with work you do every week, the same way each time. That’s where AI pays you back fastest.

How long does it take?

For using AI, it depends on how often you practice on real work. In BCG’s 2025 survey of more than 10,600 workers, regular AI use was sharply higher for people who got at least five hours of training plus in-person coaching.

For building, our students build a working tool for their own job in two weeks, some in a single weekend.

Mistakes to skip

  • Watching hours of videos first. You’ll feel informed and still not know what to do Monday morning. Learn on a real task.
  • Learning Python first. It can help later. You don’t need it to start.
  • Doing it alone. When you get stuck by yourself, it’s easy to quit. Learning next to other people (and someone who’s done it) keeps you going.
  • Building something huge first. Build step 1, get it working, then add step 2. That’s how Jennifer built hers.

Where to start

Pick one task from your week and ask yourself two questions:

  1. Do I do it every week (or every day)?
  2. Could I explain the steps to a new hire in one or a couple of detailed emails?

If you’re new to AI, use it on that task in a chat this week (step 1). If you already do that, the task is a good first build (step 2).

Frequently asked questions
Can I learn AI without being a coder?
Yes. Start by using ChatGPT or Claude on tasks from your own week, and give it context, an example and your rules. When you’re ready, tools like Claude Code let you build working tools by describing them in plain English.
Do I need to learn Python to use AI at work?
No. Python can help later if you want to understand the code AI writes, but you don’t need it to start. Most of our students have never written code.
How long does it take to learn AI without coding?
It depends on how often you use it on real work. Daily practice on tasks you already do is what makes it stick. MakerSquare students build a working tool for their own job in two weeks, some in a single weekend.
