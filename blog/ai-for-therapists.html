AI for Therapists: The Admin, Not the Therapy

Marliis Schneider September 20, 2026 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • AI note tools (generating SOAP and progress notes from sessions) save therapists 45-60 minutes a day of administrative time.
  • That time matters: more than a quarter of clinicians have reduced caseloads for admin work, and nearly half say they could see more clients with lighter documentation.
  • The line is firm and increasingly legal: several states now prohibit AI from providing therapy or making therapeutic decisions. AI handles the paperwork, not the care.
  • The right frame: AI as the assistant that takes the administrative load off the humans doing the actual therapy.

Mental health care in 2026 has a clear and careful consensus on AI: it should not act as a therapist, but it can take the administrative weight off the ones who do. The direct answer: AI's safe, valuable role for therapists is documentation and scheduling - drafting session notes and handling admin so clinicians reclaim close to an hour a day - while the therapeutic relationship and every clinical decision stay entirely human.

The administrative crisis AI actually addresses

Behavioral health is under real operational strain, and paperwork is a big part of it. More than a quarter of clinicians have reduced their caseloads over the past year to accommodate administrative work, and nearly half say they could see more patients if documentation requirements were lighter. Into that gap, AI documentation tools like Mentalyc and Upheal generate SOAP and progress notes from session recordings, saving therapists 45-60 minutes per day of administrative time. For a clinician stretched thin, that's the difference between paperwork after every session and being present for the next client.

The line - now written into law

This is a field where the boundary isn't just ethical, it's increasingly legal. In 2025 Illinois became the first state to prohibit AI from providing therapy or making therapeutic decisions, followed by Nevada and Utah. The professional consensus is unambiguous: AI should not act as a therapist. That's not a limitation to work around - it's the correct design. The therapeutic relationship, clinical judgment, and care itself are human, full stop. What AI can safely do is everything around the therapy: notes, scheduling, reminders, and admin.

There's a reason this matters beyond compliance. More than 1 in 5 Americans now use AI chatbots for mental health support, and 58% of them didn't follow up with a provider afterward - a real risk that underscores why the profession draws the line so firmly. For a practicing therapist, the responsible use of AI is unambiguous: let it handle the load that keeps you from clients, and keep the care itself yours.

What this means for therapists

Used correctly, AI gives a therapist back close to an hour a day and some breathing room in an overstretched system - without touching the work that only a clinician can do. Knowing exactly where that line sits is clinical judgment, not technical skill, which is the MakerSquare premise. Our use cases show what building administrative tools around a real practice looks like, with the human firmly in charge of the care.

AI won't do the therapy. It'll give the therapist back the hour they've been spending on notes.

Frequently asked questions
How can therapists use AI in 2026?
Safely and valuably for administration - primarily documentation (AI tools generate SOAP and progress notes from sessions, saving 45-60 minutes a day) plus scheduling, reminders, and admin. AI should not provide therapy or make clinical decisions; it handles the load around the care so clinicians have more time for clients.
Can AI replace therapists?
No - and in several states it's now illegal for AI to act as a therapist. Illinois, Nevada, and Utah have prohibited AI from providing therapy or making therapeutic decisions. The professional consensus is clear: the therapeutic relationship and clinical judgment are human. AI's role is administrative, not clinical.
How much time does AI save therapists?
AI documentation tools that generate notes from sessions save therapists about 45-60 minutes per day of administrative time. That's significant given that many clinicians have cut caseloads for paperwork and say they could see more clients with lighter documentation.
Is it ethical for therapists to use AI?
Yes, for administrative tasks used responsibly - documentation with client consent and privacy safeguards, scheduling, and admin. The ethical (and increasingly legal) line is that AI must not act as the therapist or make clinical decisions. Used to reduce paperwork rather than replace care, it's widely accepted.
Do therapists need technical skills to use AI tools?
No. AI documentation tools are built for clinical workflows and require no coding. The important judgment - where AI safely helps versus where care must stay human - comes from clinical training, not technical ability.
