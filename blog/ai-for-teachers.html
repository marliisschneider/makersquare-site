AI for Teachers: Less Prep, More Teaching

Marliis Schneider September 3, 2026 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • Teacher adoption of AI nearly doubled in a year - from 32% in 2024 to 61% in 2025 - with K-12 teachers (83%) far ahead of higher-ed faculty (22%).
  • The biggest wins are in prep, not teaching: lesson planning (38%), classroom materials (37%), and drafting parent communications are the most common uses.
  • Teachers report quality improvements from 57% (grading and feedback) to 74% (administrative work) - and most reinvest the saved time into individualized student feedback.
  • The point isn't to automate teaching. It's to automate the hours around teaching so there are more of them left for the part only a teacher can do.

Teaching has always come with a second, invisible job: the hours of planning, grading, and paperwork that happen after the students go home. That's exactly where AI for teachers is landing in 2026 - not in the classroom itself, but in the prep and admin around it. The direct answer: AI saves teachers the most time on lesson planning, generating classroom materials, grading support, and parent communication, and the best teachers are using those recovered hours to give students more individual attention, not less.

Adoption nearly doubled in a single year

The shift has been fast. Teacher adoption of AI jumped from 32% in 2024 to 61% in 2025 - a 29-point increase in one year. And it's concentrated where the workload is heaviest: 83% of K-12 teachers now use AI tools, compared to 22% of higher-education faculty. This is no longer an early-adopter story. For most K-12 teachers, using AI for prep has quietly become normal.

Where the time actually goes back

The most common uses tell you exactly where the hours are: research and content gathering (44%), creating lesson plans (38%), summarizing information (38%), and generating classroom materials (37%). Underneath the headline number, teachers most often reach for AI to brainstorm lesson ideas and to create or update lesson plans - the recurring, time-consuming prep that used to eat evenings and weekends.

And the quality holds up. Teachers report quality improvements ranging from 57% for grading and feedback to 74% for administrative work, with 16% or fewer saying quality dropped. In other words, for most teachers this isn't a trade of quality for speed - it's both.

The part that matters: what the saved time buys

Here's the number that should shape how schools think about this. When teachers save time with AI, they overwhelmingly reinvest it into individualized student feedback, more tailored lessons, parent communication, and - not trivially - their own work-life balance. The saved hours aren't disappearing into 'efficiency.' They're going back into the human parts of teaching that a tool can't do and that teachers rarely had enough time for.

That's the whole case for AI in teaching, and it's a genuinely good one: automate the drafting and the paperwork so there's more of the teacher left for the students. The technology handles the first draft of the worksheet; the teacher handles the kid who's struggling with it.

What this means for teachers building AI skills

The teachers getting the most from AI aren't the most technical - they're the ones who know their students and curriculum well enough to direct the tool precisely and catch when its output misses. That's a learnable skill. MakerSquare is built on the same idea: the people best positioned to build with AI are the ones who understand the problem deeply, not the ones who can code. The best teachers were never going to be replaced by a worksheet generator. They're going to be the ones who used it to get their evenings back.

Frequently asked questions
How are teachers using AI in 2026?
Most commonly for prep and admin: creating and updating lesson plans (38%), generating classroom materials (37%), research and content gathering (44%), summarizing information, and drafting parent communications. The heaviest uses are the recurring, time-consuming tasks that happen outside class time.
Does AI actually save teachers time?
Yes - and teachers report the quality holds up, with improvements from 57% for grading and feedback to 74% for administrative work, and 16% or fewer reporting a quality drop. Most teachers reinvest the saved time into individualized student feedback and tailored lessons rather than cutting corners.
Will AI replace teachers?
No. AI is landing in the prep and admin around teaching - lesson drafts, materials, grading support, paperwork - not in the human work of actually teaching, responding to a struggling student, or managing a classroom. It gives teachers more time for the parts a tool can't do.
What AI tools are best for teachers?
General assistants (Claude, ChatGPT) handle most lesson-planning, materials, and communication drafting well, and purpose-built education tools add grading and curriculum features. The best starting point is applying a general tool to your single most time-consuming recurring task - usually lesson planning.
Is it okay for teachers to use AI to plan lessons and grade?
Using AI to draft lesson plans, generate materials, and support grading is widely accepted and now common - 60% of educators use AI for planning and grading tasks. The key is that the teacher stays the judgment layer: reviewing, adapting to their actual students, and owning the final feedback.
