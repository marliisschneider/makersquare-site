What do you actually learn at an AI bootcamp? A week-by-week breakdown.

Marliis Schneider July 8, 2026 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • Week 1 focuses on foundations: prompting with precision, building with Cursor, and setting up the data layer for real projects.
  • Week 2 is a capstone build — you design, build, and deploy a working AI-powered tool with instructor support and peer feedback.
  • The goal isn't knowing more about AI. It's having built something real that you can point to and iterate on after you leave.
  • The skills you don't learn at a bootcamp: ML theory, model training, Python data science. Those are different programs with different goals.

"AI bootcamp" covers a wide range of programs — from weekend workshops to multi-month online courses. This breakdown covers what you should expect from a two-week, in-person, applied program designed for professionals who want to build real AI-powered tools without starting from a computer science background.

This is what MakerSquare's curriculum looks like. Specific tools and structure vary by program, but the breakdown is meant to give you a concrete picture of how a serious two-week program is sequenced.

Before you arrive

Good programs send pre-work before Day 1. At MakerSquare, students get a quickstart guide covering environment setup (installing Cursor, setting up accounts for Claude and Perplexity), foundational readings on how large language models work, and a prompt engineering primer. The goal is to arrive on Day 1 ready to build — not spending half the day troubleshooting tool installations.

Week 1: Foundations and skill-building

Days 1–2: Prompting and AI tools

The first two days cover the fundamentals of working with AI effectively. Not "here are some AI tools" — but how to write prompts that produce specific, reliable output, how to structure context, and when to trust AI output versus when to verify it. Most people arrive having used ChatGPT casually. By Day 2 they're using Claude with enough precision to get production-usable output on the first try.

Days 3–4: Building with Cursor

Cursor is an AI-powered code editor that makes software development accessible to non-engineers. Days 3 and 4 cover the core features — tab autocomplete, Cmd+K for targeted edits, and Agent mode for longer multi-step builds — with a project focus on building something real rather than completing exercises. By Day 4, most students with zero coding background have built a working web application.

Day 5: Databases and data layer

Day 5 covers Supabase — a database platform that works with Cursor and handles the data storage layer for AI-powered apps. Students connect their Cursor project to a working database, learn how to structure tables, and understand how data flows between the AI layer and the storage layer. This is often the day that makes everything click — students see how a real application is actually assembled.

Week 2: Capstone build and deployment

Days 6–8: Capstone project

Week 2 opens with students choosing and designing a capstone project — something they would actually use or that solves a real problem in their work. This is where the program design matters most. Good programs provide instructor support and peer feedback throughout the build, not just on Demo Day. The project brief is intentionally open-ended: the constraint is that it must be functional and deployable, not that it must look a specific way.

Days 9–10: Polish, deploy, and Demo Day

The final days are refinement and deployment. Students polish their projects, add edge case handling, write documentation, and deploy to a live URL. Day 10 is Demo Day — students present their projects to each other, guest professionals, and the instructor team. The presentations are short and focused on the problem solved and the tool built, not on slides or theory.

What you walk away with

A working AI-powered project you built — deployed and live. The ability to prompt AI tools with the specificity required to get reliable output on real work. A working understanding of how AI applications are structured. A peer cohort of professionals doing similar work, which tends to be underrated as a resource. And the foundation to keep building after you leave, without needing a developer or a course.

What you won't learn

Machine learning theory, model training, Python data science, or research-level AI. Those are different programs with different audiences. An applied AI bootcamp is for people who want to use and build with AI tools — not for people who want to build the models themselves. If the goal is ML research or data science, a traditional computer science or data science program is the right fit.

Frequently asked questions
What do you learn at an AI bootcamp?
At a well-structured AI bootcamp, you learn practical AI tools (Claude, Cursor, Perplexity), prompt engineering and system design, database setup, automation workflows, and how to build and deploy a real AI-powered project. The emphasis is on doing rather than theory — you build something real in the first week and ship it in the second.
Do I need coding experience to attend an AI bootcamp?
Not for a program focused on applied AI skills. MakerSquare is designed for operators, founders, and knowledge workers — most attendees have zero prior coding experience. The tools taught (especially Cursor) are designed to make building accessible to non-engineers. Some programs do require programming prerequisites, so it's worth checking the curriculum before enrolling.
What do you walk away with from an AI bootcamp?
At minimum: a working AI-powered project you built yourself, the skill to prompt AI tools with enough specificity to get reliable output, a working understanding of when to trust AI and when to verify it, and a peer cohort of professionals doing similar work. The project is the most important output — it's the thing that transfers back to your actual job.
How is an AI bootcamp different from an online AI course?
Online courses teach concepts. Bootcamps build skill. The difference shows up in completion rates (online courses average 5–15% completion; in-person programs are far higher) and in what transfers to work. The research on structured in-person learning consistently shows better outcomes for complex skill development than self-paced alternatives.
