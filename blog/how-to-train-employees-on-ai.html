For Companies

How to train employees on AI — without hiring engineers.

Marliis Schneider July 28, 2026 5 min read
Key takeaways
  • Your best AI builders are already on payroll — they just don’t know it yet
  • Start with one team and one recurring workflow, not a company-wide rollout
  • The goal of week one is a working tool, not a slide deck
  • Engineers are for deep custom systems — your first ten wins don’t need one

Every week we talk to Austin business owners who believe the same thing: to “do AI,” they need to hire technical people. It’s the most expensive misconception in small business right now — and it’s backwards.

The person who should automate your invoice processing is the person who processes your invoices. They know every exception, every vendor quirk, every step that eats an afternoon. An engineer would spend weeks in discovery learning what your ops person already knows. The building part — the part that used to require the engineer — is the part that just got easy.

Why domain knowledge beats coding skill now

Tools like Claude Code changed what “building” means. A working AI agent — one that reads incoming email, drafts responses, and files what matters into your systems — is now something a non-technical operator can build in days, not something a team of developers builds in a quarter. What the tools can’t supply is knowing which work is worth automating. That knowledge lives in your people.

We watched this play out in our first cohort. An executive leadership consultant and a SaaS account executive — neither with a coding background — each left two weeks later with a live website, a working AI agent, and an automated workflow, all built on their own real work. One of them put it plainly afterward: he was scared it wouldn’t apply to his work. “It definitely is — I’d recommend it to everyone.”

The sequence that works

1. Pick one team, not the whole company. The team drowning in the most repetitive computer work goes first — usually ops or sales. A company-wide AI initiative produces committees; one team with a mandate produces results other teams get jealous of. Jealousy is your rollout plan.

2. Pick one recurring workflow. Not the most impressive automation — the most repetitive one. Meeting notes that need logging. Invoices that need entering. Reports that get assembled from the same three sources every Monday. The best first project is boring, frequent, and measurable.

3. Build to working, not to trained. This is where most corporate AI training fails: it ends with certificates instead of tools. Set the bar at “running on our real data by the last day.” If the training ends and nothing changed in how the team works Monday morning, you bought a lecture.

4. Let it compound. Once one person on the team can build, every new annoyance becomes a candidate. The second tool costs a fraction of the first — the skills are already in the building. This is the real argument for training over outsourcing: a consultant’s know-how leaves with the consultant. A trained team keeps compounding.

What this looks like at full scale

RoverPass, an Austin reservation company, runs a live AI ops command center that tracks hundreds of campgrounds in real time — watching for exceptions and routing what matters to a human. It wasn’t built by an outside firm. It was built inside the business, by people who understand the business, on the same stack we teach. We wrote up how it works.

Where to start this month

Run this diagnostic in your next leadership meeting: ask each team lead, “what does your team do every week that a smart intern could do with perfect instructions?” Write the answers down. That list — not a vendor pitch — is your AI roadmap. Then give one team the skills to work through it. In person, hands-on, on your real workflows — that’s exactly what we build with Austin companies, and your team leaves with the first items on that list already running.
