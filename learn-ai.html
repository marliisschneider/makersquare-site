Learning path

How to learn AI: an honest path for beginners who are serious about it.

Marliis Schneider Updated November 4, 2026 14 min read
The short answer

The best way to learn AI as a working adult is to use it on your real work every day, in this order: prompting, a paid chat assistant like Claude or ChatGPT, Claude Code, a website you put online, an agent, and an automation. Start free. Add structure when you stall. Most people need 60 to 100 hours of hands-on practice to get through all six. Self-study spreads that over four to six months. A full-time in-person program compresses it into two weeks.

Search "how to learn AI" and most of what comes back is a roadmap for becoming a machine learning engineer: learn Python, then linear algebra, then statistics, then neural networks. That advice is correct for that goal. It is the wrong advice for most people asking the question.

We teach non-technical professionals to build with AI: lawyers, sales reps, operators, founders, people changing careers. This page is the path we would give a friend. It covers what to learn, in what order, how long each step really takes, what to skip, and how to choose between free, online, and in-person learning. We run an in-person program, and it is one option at the end. For a lot of readers, the free path is the right one.

First, decide which kind of "learning AI" you mean

There are two different skills hiding under the same search, and they take very different amounts of time.

Path A
Use and build with AI tools

You want AI to do real work for you: draft, research, analyze, build a website, run a workflow, act as an agent. No math. Little or no traditional coding. Realistic time to real skill: 2 weeks full-time or 4 to 6 months part-time.

For: managers, operators, founders, marketers, lawyers, finance people, career changers. This page is for you.

Path B
Build AI models

You want to train and tune models, work as an ML engineer, or do research. That means Python, linear algebra, probability, and deep learning frameworks. Realistic time: 12 to 24 months of steady study, more without a technical background.

Start with fast.ai's Practical Deep Learning (free) or Andrew Ng's Machine Learning Specialization on Coursera.

If you are not sure, you are almost certainly Path A. Path B people usually already know they want to write Python every day. Everything below is Path A.

The path at a glance

Most people who end up good at this move through four stages. You do not have to do all four. You do have to do them in this order, because each one only works if the one before it is done.

StageWhat it gets youRealistic timeTypical cost
1. Free self-studyDaily habits with a chat assistant, basic prompting2 to 4 weeks, 3 to 5 hrs/week$0 to $20/month
2. Structured courseA complete map of the tools, fewer blind spots2 to 6 weeks$0 to $500
3. Real projectsThings that run: a site, an agent, an automation1 to 3 months part-time$20 to $100/month in tools
4. Intensive or in-personSpeed, feedback, and a finished project you can show3 days to 2 weeks full-time$1,200 to $25,000

Stage 4 is optional. Some people go straight from stage 1 to stage 4 because they know they will not finish alone. Others never need it. We cover how to decide further down.

What to learn, in what order

Six skills, each building on the last. For each one: what it is, how long it realistically takes, and how you know you are done.

1
Prompting: giving AI clear instructions

Prompting is writing a good brief. Say who the AI should act as, what you want, what good looks like, and what to avoid. Give it your real material: the email thread, the spreadsheet, the contract. Then ask it to ask you questions before it starts.

Time: 1 to 2 weeks of daily use. You do not need a course for this. You need 20 real tasks.

You are done when you can get a usable first draft of a real work document in three tries or fewer.

Read: How to write prompts that work and our prompt examples.

2
Claude or ChatGPT as a daily work tool

Pick one and pay for it. Claude Pro and ChatGPT Plus are both $20 a month, and the paid tiers are where the useful features live: file uploads, longer documents, projects that remember your context, and deep research. Learn to set up a project with your own reference documents so you stop re-explaining your job every session.

Time: 2 to 4 weeks, overlapping with step 1.

You are done when you reach for it before you reach for a search engine, and at least one weekly task takes half as long as it used to.

Read: Claude vs. ChatGPT vs. Gemini for work and the free Claude quickstart.

3
Claude Code: AI that works on files and builds things

This is where you move from using AI to building with it. Claude Code is Anthropic's coding agent. You describe what you want in plain English, and it writes and edits files, runs commands, and tells you what it did. You are the person who decides what to build and checks that it works. You need a paid Claude plan to use it.

Time: one weekend (6 to 10 hours) to your first working build. Four to six weeks of regular use to feel comfortable. The terminal is the scary part for most people, and it stops being scary after about an hour.

You are done when you have built three small things that you use, without copying a tutorial line by line.

Read: Learn Claude Code: a beginner's guide for non-engineers. If you prefer a visual editor, how to use Cursor as a non-coder covers the same idea in an app.

4
Build a website and put it online

A project is not real until it has a URL. Build a simple site with Claude Code, save it to GitHub, and deploy it on Vercel. Both have free tiers. Once that works, add a database with Supabase so your site can store sign-ups, orders, or records. This step teaches you how real software is put together, which makes every later step easier.

Time: 1 to 2 days for your first live site. Another week to add a database and logins.

You are done when you can send someone a link to something you built and it works on their phone.

Read: How to build a website with AI, then the guides to git for non-coders, deploying with Vercel and GitHub, and setting up Supabase.

5
Agents: AI that takes steps on its own

An agent is an AI that has tools and decides which to use. It can search, read your files, call an API, and loop until a job is done. Start with one narrow, boring job: research ten companies and fill in a spreadsheet, or read incoming requests and draft replies for you to approve. Broad "do everything" agents fail. Narrow ones work.

Time: 2 to 4 weeks part-time after steps 3 and 4.

You are done when an agent finishes one real task end to end and you trust the output enough to check it instead of redo it.

Read: How to build an AI agent and how to set up a CLAUDE.md file, which is how you give an agent standing instructions.

6
Automations: work that runs without you

An automation is an agent or script that runs on a schedule or when something happens: a new email, a form submission, Monday at 8am. This is where AI starts saving hours per week instead of minutes per task. You can do this with visual tools like Zapier or Make, or by having Claude Code write it. Pick one approach and stick with it for a month.

Time: 2 to 4 weeks part-time.

You are done when something you used to do by hand every week now happens on its own, and you get a note when it fails.

Read: AI automation for non-engineers.

Why this order? Each step reuses the one before it. Agents are prompting plus tools. Automations are agents plus a trigger. A website teaches you files, git, and deployment, which is how agents and automations get shipped. People who jump to agents first usually come back to step 1 a month later.

How long it really takes to learn AI

The honest number is 60 to 100 hours of hands-on practice to get through all six steps at a working level. Watching videos does not count toward that number. Building does.

PaceHours per weekCalendar time for steps 1 to 6
Evenings and weekends4 to 54 to 6 months, if you don't stall
Serious side project102 to 3 months
Full-time intensive30 to 402 weeks, then a few weeks of practice

The hours are about the same either way. What changes is calendar time and your odds of finishing. Self-paced learning has a well-documented completion problem. A 2019 study in Science of MIT and Harvard's online courses on edX found that about 3% of people who enrolled finished. Most people who stall do not stall because the material is hard. They stall in week three, when work gets busy and nobody notices they stopped.

One more honest note: you will not be "done." The tools change every few months. The goal is to get past the point where a new tool scares you. After step 6, picking up a new one takes an afternoon.

What to skip

Knowing what not to learn saves more time than any course. If you are on Path A, skip these:

Math and Python before you build anything. You do not need linear algebra to use Claude Code, the same way you do not need to understand combustion to drive. If you later want Path B, you can add it.
Long "how AI works" theory courses. Two hours of background is useful. Twenty hours is procrastination. One good explainer video is enough to start.
Certificate collecting. Employers and clients care what you built. A live link beats a badge.
Lists of 500 prompts. Copying someone else's prompts does not teach you to write a good brief for your own work.
Trying every new tool. A new model or app launches every week. Pick one chat assistant and one build tool, learn them well, and switch only when you hit a wall.
Learning three automation platforms. Zapier, Make, and n8n solve the same problem. Pick one approach.
Building a chatbot nobody asked for. Build something for a task you, or someone you know, does every week. Real users give real feedback.

A 30-day starter plan for beginners

If you want to start this week, here is the first month. Plan on about 4 to 5 hours a week.

Week 1
Prompt on real work

Use a free Claude or ChatGPT account on three tasks you already do: an email, a summary, a plan. Rewrite each prompt until the output is usable.

Week 2
Go paid, set up a project

Upgrade one assistant to the $20 plan. Create a project with your role, your company, and three reference documents. Use it daily.

Week 3
Install Claude Code

Follow the beginner's guide. Build a one-page website about something you know well.

Week 4
Ship it and pick the next problem

Put the site online with Vercel and GitHub. Write down one weekly task you would hand to an agent. That is month two.

How to pick a format

The content is mostly the same everywhere. What you are buying when you pay for a course is structure, feedback, and a deadline. Pick the cheapest format that gets you to the end.

FormatCostBest forWatch out for
Free self-study$0 to $20/monthSelf-starters, people testing whether AI matters for their workNo one notices when you stop
Self-paced online course$0 to $500Filling a specific gap, learning on your own scheduleLow completion; lots of watching, little building
Live online cohort$500 to $2,500People who need a schedule but can't travelEasy to keep your camera off and fall behind
In-person workshop (1 to 3 days)$1,200 to $2,500A fast start on one skill, like shipping a first appToo short to cover all six steps
In-person intensive (1 to 2 weeks)$3,999 to $25,000People who want all six steps and a finished project, fastYou need the time off, plus travel if it isn't local

Three questions settle it for most people:

1
Have you started a self-paced course before and not finished?
If yes, another self-paced course will probably end the same way. Pay for structure: a live cohort or an in-person program.
2
Do you have a specific thing you want to build?
If yes, pick a program where you build your own project, not a class exercise. If no, stay with free self-study for a month until you find one.
3
Can you take one or two weeks away from work?
If yes, an intensive is the fastest route by far. If no, a live online cohort or a weekend workshop fits better.

Free resources worth your time

You can get through steps 1 to 3 without paying for anything except a $20 assistant plan. These are the free materials we point people to:

Anthropic's free courses. Short courses on using Claude well, plus Claude Code 101 once you reach step 3.
AI for Everyone by Andrew Ng. About six hours of plain-English background. Free to audit on Coursera. This is the one theory course worth taking.
Elements of AI. A free course from the University of Helsinki on what AI can and cannot do, with no math required.
MakerSquare guides. Step-by-step walkthroughs for Claude Code, Cursor, git, Vercel, and Supabase, written for people who have never coded.
Your own backlog. Keep a list of tasks at work that annoy you. Each week, hand one to AI. This teaches more than any course on this list.

Questions people ask about learning AI

Is AI hard to learn?

Using and building with AI tools is not hard. It is unfamiliar. The first hour in a terminal feels uncomfortable, and most people are past it by the end of a weekend. Building AI models from scratch is hard, and that path takes a year or more of math and Python.

How long does it take to learn AI?

For a non-technical professional, about 60 to 100 hours of hands-on practice gets you from first prompts to running your own agents and automations. Part-time at 4 to 5 hours a week, that is 4 to 6 months. A full-time intensive compresses it into two weeks.

Do I need to know how to code or do math to learn AI?

No, unless you want to become a machine learning engineer. To use and build with AI tools, you need to write clear instructions and check the results. Tools like Claude Code write the code. Our post on how to learn AI without coding goes deeper.

What is the best AI tool to learn first?

Start with a paid plan for one chat assistant, Claude or ChatGPT, at $20 a month. Once that is a daily habit, learn Claude Code. Those two cover most of what a non-technical professional will build.

Can I learn AI for free?

Mostly. The courses and guides above are free, and free tiers of Claude and ChatGPT cover step 1. To go further you will want a paid assistant plan at about $20 a month, which Claude Code requires. Hosting a first project on Vercel and Supabase is free.
